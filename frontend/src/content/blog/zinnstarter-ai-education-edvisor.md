## Are you confused about the role AI is playing in your education?

Most students are being encouraged to use AI tools, such as Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS), to help them learn and perform in school. As a result, students, teachers, and other stakeholders must understand the nuances that arise when they rely on AI-generated outputs to complete schoolwork.

The IDEAS Club has interviewed two individuals with expertise in the field.

To better understand this subject, we first interviewed Shariq Shah, founder of [Edvisor.AI](http://edvisor.ai), an ITS Platform that endeavors to replace the traditional in-class textbook. Our second interview is with Sumeet Syal, a professor in the Department of Marketing and Business Analytics. He founded a Start-up consulting firm that helps entrepreneurs navigate their market and align decisions with the start-up’s goals and values. ## Context for Edvisor

Shariq was among the first to identify a major problem rupturing schools and academia across the country: textbook accessibility. When speaking to Shariq, he attributed this to a larger issue:

> "The problem is that many course curricula are ill-suited to the circumstances students face today. To iterate, I've broken down the issue into three different problem areas: 1) Accessibility, 2) Affordability, and 3) Curriculum Governance."

First, let us examine textbook accessibility in greater detail. In February, the National Association of College Stores conducted a nationwide survey of school faculty. They found that 63% of faculty cited textbook accessibility as a top priority. This indicates that textbook costs are a growing barrier to student learning and achievement. But textbooks aren't the only course materials learners have to worry about financing; some courses require lab fees, physical materials, digital grading systems, digital homework assignments, and libraries' printing fees.

The root of the issue is the cost of learning materials. A 2022 study found that 54% of students skip buying required textbooks, 36% report lower grades because they don't have the textbook, and 20% report failing a course because they cannot afford the required materials. Research shows that the cost of educational materials directly impedes student success. If students struggle to even afford the basic, bare-bones materials required to participate in a course, the content they're supposed to learn is neglected, inevitably leading to academic integrity issues.

Shariq states that these are symptoms of a deeper problem in the curriculum. Many proponents of curriculum overhaul argue that traditional education methods are becoming less viable as our economy becomes increasingly digital. This is because digital systems now offer more engaging ways to learn, rendering most traditional textbook curricula obsolete. Shariq supports the idea that this could ripple into students' future careers, as we may be heading toward a future where most degrees will no longer have the same value.

So, what can we do to discourage reliance on AI while revitalizing our curriculum to meet students' ever-shifting needs? The school system needs to embrace AI while promoting critical engagement with course content. This is where Edvisor comes into play: the platform uses AI to create and publish digital textbooks that students can rely on at a discount compared to hardcover books. Instructors can use this tool to enter their syllabus and build interactive course materials with audio, video, and AI Bots. Teachers also gain something valuable: time! The assignments it generates can be automatically graded while also delivering instant feedback to students via AI Chatbots. In simple terms, Edvisor replaces the in-class textbook, its content, and its assignments, saving students money and instructors' time.

Professors from Berkeley to Rhode Island and beyond rave about Edvisor and how it has improved their classrooms and redefined their students' relationship with learning. Our campus, San Jose State University, has served as the center of Edvisor's adoption. Additionally, their website features numerous positive reviews from professors at universities in Fresno, Arizona, and Kansas City, Missouri. Each entry highlights Edvisor's contribution to enhancing the learning and teaching experience in classrooms across the country.

## Prompt Engineering

Prompt engineering is a skill students will find integral to their advancement in an AI-assisted classroom. Prompting is the process of crafting effective inputs (prompts) to guide large language models (LLMs) to make a desired output. Shariq iterates the importance of prompt engineering skills for AI adoption in learning:

> "Students and instructors should develop experience in prompt engineering, while the course structure should be revised to train students and faculty in the effective use of AI."

A good prompt not only elicits relevant information but also asks for an analysis formatted to suit a student's established learning style. Edvisor AI assesses students' pre-existing knowledge and performance to deliver a catered explanation of the subject. If students need further clarification, they can adjust their prompt to address their knowledge gaps. After this process, the AI output is clearer, more holistic, more concise, or more detailed, depending on the learner's needs.

In fact, Edvisor AI goes a long way toward ensuring that all generated responses enhance learners' understanding, regardless of the pre-existing knowledge they bring. During our conversation, Shariq illustrated this with an exciting example:

> "On Edvisor, you can ask AI questions in relation to what you already know. But let's say that I am a new student who isn't that knowledgeable yet. I can input a prompt asking the AI to explain a concept using terms and concepts I am already familiar with, even if they're not academic. On the screen, I am on the dashboard for an English course, and I ask for a synopsis of the chapter on semantics explained using Pokémon. The AI generates a response that uses names and phrases in Pokémon and how their meaning are established by the roots, prefixes, and suffixes contained in the phrase."

## Oral Defenses

When speaking with Shariq, he highlighted an upcoming update that would provide a self-reflection system with a unique twist. Oral Defenses, set to release in February 2026, is an attribute directly tied to student submissions. Instructors can assign these to students for completion after their initial work is due. Shariq states:

> "When assigned, students must defend, explain, and expand upon their submissions. This feature would help Edvisor users reinforce key information and encourage students to engage in conversations about their knowledge base. If students become stumped, they will be incentivized to go back and review past course content and come back after reflecting."

As instructors read their submitted Oral Defenses, they will gain insights into how their students comprehend the material, the process they underwent to think critically, and the learning outcomes they achieved. In turn, this ensures the high-quality, precise relevance of student responses is upheld across all Edvisor submissions.

## Data Security

Since AI collects data, the main hindrance to its adoption in the education system is concerns about data security. To support teachers as they navigate AI integration, district leaders have provided MIT (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)-developed resources that include real-life examples of successful AI integration in classrooms within the district that have been early adopters of the technology.

A lot is at stake; if security standards are not upheld, a breach could leak sensitive information about individuals and educational institutions. With AI integration posing such a risk, it is imperative that both students and teachers are given counseling on how to interact with AI safely. A recent UNESCO (United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organization) survey found that fewer than 10% of schools nationwide have implemented guidance or rules for using generative AI.

In particular, a lack of guidance on data security and privacy has slowed the adoption of Intelligent Tutoring Systems, which use student performance data to fine-tune content delivery and provide tailored feedback. The Ed-Tech sector must build a foundation for AI integration, but low trust and understandable concerns have slowed uptake. Worries about algorithmic bias and data privacy are key challenges to address so that adaptive learning technologies can be integrated safely and equitably.

Fortunately, in Edvisor's case, those concerns are not warranted. This is because Edvisor has robust privacy and security measures in place to protect data, aligned with FERPA. We recognize that educators may be on edge about this subject, but they can rest assured that Edvisor takes student, teacher, and school privacy seriously. Shariq iterates:

> "The app doesn't share personal user data with the AI. In other words, the AI tool doesn't need to know personally identifiable information about students or teachers (such as names, birthdates, or sex). Learners and Instructors are protected from leaks that could expose their personal data, as Edvisor mitigates the risk by nipping it in the bud. This is why all Edvisor users can benefit from AI-enhanced learning while remaining anonymous to the servers that host the data gleaned from their interactions."

## The Use of The A* Algorithm

In IDEAS’ previous blog post, Shariq, the founder of Edvisor: Online Textbook Publisher, helped me to wrap my head around how Intelligent Tutoring Systems work. To better understand how platforms like Edvisor use AI to personalize learning and enhance interactivity, Shariq pointed me to the A* algorithm. This algorithm has wide-ranging applications across industries and fields. It finds the best route (lowest cost) from start to finish, using the most efficient method to accomplish the objective. In education and Intelligent Tutoring Systems, the algorithm identifies the best "path" through the curriculum and estimates the effort required to reach learning goals from a student's current state.

To predict the next best teaching step or hint, the system adapts the learning path in real time. In particular, student models that incorporate knowledge and misconception data are used to draw inferences. The system uses a heuristic technique, a problem-solving approach that draws connections from given data to related information.

While interviewing Sumeet for this blog post, I gave him an overview of the algorithm and its objective, as well as how ITS systems can leverage it. In a goal-oriented format, A* finds optimal sequences to find and adjust the shortest path. In an educational setting, the algorithm supports effective learning by evaluating options and selecting the most promising choice. The deployment of the A* algorithm relies upon mechanisms that are core to Intelligent Tutoring Systems, such as:

- Tracking students' knowledge, errors, and progress to provide context for the model.
- Incorporating knowledge of the topic or subject sourced from experts.
- The "tutor" logic decides what to teach and how to provide hints and feedback.
- An Interface Model handles communication through text and UX/UI visuals.

Edvisor applies the A* algorithm in numerous areas, giving multiple examples of it in action within an Intelligent Tutoring System:

- Adaptive Instruction: The difficulty of the content is adjusted based on real-time performance
- Error Diagnosis: Mistakes are highlighted as they occur, with remedial actions or targeted feedback provided.
- Simulations & Gamification: Interactive scenarios are used to garner deeper engagement.

**Jupjeet:** “I have 3 questions to ask you regarding the integration of the A* algorithm to enhance learning. I’ll ask you that one at a time:<br>1.) How should A* be explained to students/teachers so they understand how it impacts their learning?”

> **Sumeet:** “Students and instructors must understand that AI will not give 100% accuracy in its responses. Using AI for learning can enhance critical thinking, but overreliance on it can stifle creativity.
>
> One insight I’d like to offer for ponderance is that AI doesn't know everything you need from it; it only knows what you ask it. And it’s not great at discerning which prompts produce valuable outputs and which don't. Out of 100 prompts, 90% produce similar responses, but the remaining 10% are overlooked, even though they offer the most thought-provoking insights. As a result, AI forgets the creativity displayed by the minority of questions, and its overall data bandwidth opts to focus on the mediocre 90%.”

**Jupjeet:** “2.) Is there a way to view the decision tree created by A* to inform how/why their curriculum was delivered as it was?”

> **Sumeet:** “Yes, it is 100% possible to visualize the A* pipeline into a data-based decision tree, and I completely agree that ITS platforms should provide these. We need to mitigate the risk of students falling into the trap of overly trusting AI for efficient outputs.
>
> Students should be able to tweak the AI Charts, using them as a starting point for their learning. They ought to serve as a sketch of the content students need to focus on, leaving it to the learner to flesh out a full skeleton of their own understanding. This will also incentivize them to consult with professors and peers to compare, contrast, and seek advice on how to tailor their algorithm to optimize their education.”

**Jupjeet:** “3.) Can an A* algorithm be altered/tailored to customize instruction in cases where it's found that the algorithm isn't providing the best possible decision?”

> **Sumeet:** “Students understand their own learning needs and performance best, so they are better suited than any algorithmic model to custom-tailor their learning path. As such, ITS platforms need to implement features that enable students to take the initiative to do so.”

## Establish guiding principles

If a lecturer already has such a rubric, they can effortlessly upload it to Edvisor and have all assignments graded against it.

During our discussion, I asked Sumeet for advice on developing a rubric like this. We need a call for edtech specialists, teachers, students, and school districts to come together and develop common guiding principles for students and teachers on using generative AI. This would define the purpose and scope of generative AI in the classroom, outlining areas where it should be prioritized and cases where it should be limited or monitored.

When implementing a rubric of guiding principles at the district level, ITS platforms enable the district to create, manage, and deploy customizable tools. All one has to do is upload their own curriculum guides, handbooks, or district policies and convert them into AI assistants that generate responses tailored to their district's context. Administrators can receive real-time alerts when improper use is detected. AI-generated content can be controlled while upholding unique values and standards by actively adjusting moderation rules across a wide range of contexts.

However, students found that checking out a 20-year-old book from the library was less beneficial than searching a search engine for the latest research article.

To do this, I’d recommend they find the most innovative AI users who want to help humanity, people with a moral compass who aren't biased toward or invested in companies. These arbiters of regulation must look at the issues plaguing the education system objectively and holistically, without profit incentives that create conflicts of interest.

## Self-Reflection System

How did they validate the data by citing sources to show critical thinking/discernment of credibility?<br>3.) Reflect on how they used/prompted AI and what was done improperly. What should they do differently next time to make it a more efficient process and produce a better output/project?
>
> When they ponder these questions in order, it simulates optimizing an ML pipeline, giving students experience assessing and modifying a workflow (with AI integrated to complement human work and accomplish tasks) to best serve their needs for a particular class, assignment, case, or scenario. Thus, this kind of self-reflection system poses value by being translatable into a contemporary work environment with operations that integrate AI systems and ML models.”
